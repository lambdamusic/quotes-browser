The singularity as existential risk

The last few years have produced an increasing amount of popular discourse about the singularity, which describes events in technological development that would lead to human-level machine intelligence, and would lead to computers achieving superintelligence. Following this occurrence, there would come an explosion of intelligent machines with cognition so advanced that it poses an immediate existential risk to humanity. Machine intelligence would learn how to improve independently of human programmers; it would spawn other machines capable of improving their intelligences, resulting, some argue, in the end of the human race.

The hard problem of consciousness

Many researchers in AI and neuroscience choose not to grapple with what philosopher David Chalmers calls the “hard problem” of consciousness. The hard problem refers to subjective or phenomenal experience. Chalmers argues that solving perception or cognition is easy because we know how to study these problems, and can explain them with physical mechanisms that perform these functions. But, he says, even when we have explained all the functions of perception15 with physical mechanisms, we are still left with the question of why any of those functions is associated with a subjective experience. When we see a cup of coffee, “there is something it is like” to see a cup of coffee.16 We feel something, and we experience it. How and why? Nobody knows.

Four generals for Byzantine fault tolerance

Leibniz withdraws his binary essay

Leibniz came up with binary numbers in about 1679, when he wrote a letter to a colleague about a “new science of numbers” on which he was working. Leibniz was soon invited by the Royal Academy of Sciences in Paris to contribute to their new annual publication, the Histoire de l’Académie Royale des Sciences, which was more or less the equivalent of being published in the high-impact journals Science or Nature today. Leibniz was excited by the invitation and felt that, of all that he was working on, the concept of binary numbers was by far the most important. He wrote “Essay d’une Nouvelle Science des Nombres”—the first scientific paper ever on binary numbers—in 1701. The Royal Academy expressed interest in the idea, but they could not comprehend what binary numbers could be used for and now awaited “its applications with impatience.” In other words, they asked Leibniz to revise his essay because binary numbers seemed like a neat idea with no practical use. Leibniz realized that he would not have time to elaborate on his calculating machine before the publication deadline and, amazingly, wrote the Royal Academy asking them to withdraw his essay, which likely would have been published regardless. Observer-independent and observer-relative facts

Searle makes a distinction between observer-independent features and observer-relative features. Observer-independent features are things like gravity, mass, chemical bonds,48 the solar system, and mountains. These things exist even in the absence of human minds. Observer-relative features are things like the United States of America, that football is a game with eleven players per side, and that a green piece of paper with cultish imagery and “twenty dollars” written on it is money. These things require a society of conscious agents believing in them to exist. A rough-and-ready test for whether or not a fact is observer independent is this: Could it have existed if there had been no conscious agents at all? Reality became a problem after LSD

Hofmann says: “Reality became for me a problem after my experience with LSD. Before, I had believed there was only one reality, the reality of everyday life. Just one true reality and the rest was imagination and was not real. But under the influence of LSD, I entered into realities which were as real and even more real than the one of everyday. And I thought about the nature of reality and I got some deeper insights.” This to me indicates that LSD has the ability to create awareness of different kinds of perceptions, in which we encounter the fact that our perception of everyday life is a creation of our minds. Hofmann continues: “I analyzed the mechanisms involved in the production of the normal world view that we call the ‘everyday reality.’ What are the factors that constitute it? What is inside about what is outside? What comes from the outside in and what is just inside?” If we adopt the hallucinogenic model of consciousness, the entire contents of your subjective awareness come from just inside. What comes in from outside is merely a constraint on what the inside reality can look and feel like.

Bicycle Day and the origin of the trip

Soon after he ingested the drug, his entire world began to transform—the colors, the forms—and he felt his ego begin to dissolve. Hofmann grew afraid he had taken too much, and asked his assistant to help him home by bicycle. While trying to bike home, Hofmann pedaled and pedaled but he had the feeling that he could not move from the spot. Time seemed to stand still. This is how the bicycle trip came to be used as a way to refer to the LSD experience as a “trip.” Temporal integration of brain processes into a unified conscious experience is one of the most mysterious aspects of consciousness, and LSD directly perturbs this process.

LSD is less harmful than alcohol

The law did not ban the use of LSD in research, but now researchers would be required to receive approval from the Bureau of Narcotics and Dangerous Drugs, which would later become the DEA. Following suit, the UN classified several mind-altering drugs as Schedule I in 1971. This essentially stopped worldwide research using LSD. As a Schedule I drug, LSD is said to have no medical purpose, to have the potential to lead to addiction, or to have the potential to cause harm. This is despite the fact that on a drug-harm score developed in the United Kingdom, which sought to assess whether a drug causes harm to self or others, LSD is ten times less harmful to users than alcohol. What’s more, LSD is fifty times less harmful to others—people not even using the drug—than alcohol. In other words, there is no evidence that people get high on LSD and beat their spouses or cause car accidents; the same cannot be said for alcohol.

Psychedelics as tools to study consciousness

Despite revolutionary advancements in brain imaging over the last twenty years, there exist few studies on LSD and hallucinogens using modern neuroscience techniques. Researchers are now able to measure various aspects of the neural mechanisms of LSD action using objective methods. However, exactly how the interaction of LSD with neurotransmitters leads to profound alteration of our subjective conscious experience is a mystery. This is partly due to our poor understanding of what consciousness is and how it exists in the first place. Psychedelic drugs perturb consciousness in a unique and novel way, and they are therefore powerful tools with which to study the brain mechanisms underlying consciousness. The relative lack of modern neuroscientific research on LSD and hallucinogens means that the neural mechanisms mediating all of the incredible effects of psychedelics remain largely unknown. This is changing, however, and a team of brain scientists in the United Kingdom led by Robin Carhart-Harris and David Nutt are intensely investigating how the brain reacts to psychedelics. One thing has been established: all psychedelics are serotonin 5-HT2A receptor agonists. What does this mean?
